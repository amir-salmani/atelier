# Originality — mechanism, not dressing

A library of teardowns makes copying easy. That is the whole risk of building
one, and it is why this document is a gate rather than a note.

## The one distinction everything rests on

Every admired thing splits into three parts. Write all three down or you cannot
tell which one you are taking.

| | | |
|---|---|---|
| **Mechanism** | What it does structurally or temporally | *"The accent word arrives alone, ~200ms before the sentence containing it"* |
| **Dressing** | The specific type, colour, illustration, copy, layout, imagery | *Suisse Int'l, pink `#FF488B`, "scales", bottom-left on near-black* |
| **Reason** | What about **that** product's audience and content made it right | *A one-line value claim whose whole argument is one verb* |

**Mechanism may be taken. Dressing may never be. And mechanism only transfers
if the reason survives the swap.**

The word-first reveal in the example above works because that headline turns on
a single verb. Applied to a headline with no load-bearing word, the same
mechanism produces an arbitrary word flashing on a dark screen — the mechanism
travelled and the reason did not.

## The four tests

Run them in this order. Each can fail, and a failure is a finding, not a note.

### 1 · The swap test

Put your content into their design. **If it still looks right, you copied.**
Their layout was fitted to their content; if yours drops in cleanly, you took
the fitting rather than the thinking.

Then the other direction: apply their mechanism to your content. **If the result
looks nothing like their page, you learned.** That is the pass condition.

### 2 · The three-source rule

Every element in a direction must trace to either:

- **two or more unrelated teardowns**, or
- **a constraint of this product** — its content, audience, brand, budget or
  a rule in the entity's book.

An element tracing to exactly one source is a lift, however well-argued.
`inspire`'s provenance table exists to make this checkable at a glance rather
than defensible in prose.

### 3 · The inversion

For every borrowed mechanism, state its opposite and why it was rejected.

*"Stagger the reveal at 150ms"* → the opposite is *"everything arrives at once."*
Why not? If the answer is *"staggering feels better"*, you did not choose, you
defaulted — and a default borrowed from someone else's site is a clone with
extra steps.

Some inversions win. Keep at least one site in your library whose answer to
*"what moves?"* is *"almost nothing"* — you will need something that disagrees
with the rest of the shelf.

### 4 · The subtraction pass

Before a direction is proposed, remove its most distinctive element and read it
again. **If it still works, that element was decoration** — and decoration
borrowed from a teardown is the exact failure this document exists to prevent.
If it collapses, the element is structural and has earned its place.

## What is never taken, under any argument

- A **layout**, a **headline structure**, an **identity**, an **illustration
  style**, a **colour system**, or a **name**.
- A **typeface chosen because they chose it.** And never a trial licence — a
  teardown will tell you when a site shipped one, and it is not a licence to
  follow.
- A **section sequence.** The order a page makes its argument in is the argument.

## The honest part

Most directions will not pass all four tests, and the useful output of running
them is usually *"this is one mechanism and two pieces of dressing"* — at which
point you keep the mechanism and go and do the rest of the work.

A direction that passes all four on the first attempt is more likely to mean the
tests were run generously than that the work was original. Run the subtraction
pass again.
